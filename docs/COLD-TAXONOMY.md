# Cold Taxonomy

*A visual philosophy. Not a style guide — a way of looking, from which style follows.*

---

Cold Taxonomy is the discipline of classifying what refuses to be classified. It borrows the whole
apparatus of the naturalist's plate — the specimen isolated on its ground, the measuring rule laid
beside it, the numbered callout, the catalogue mark inked in the corner — and turns that apparatus
on subjects that have no business submitting to it. The apparatus is not irony. It is belief. The
hand that draws these things believes utterly that they can be measured, and it is that belief,
held steadily against a subject that will not hold still, which produces the tension the whole
movement runs on. Every plate must look as though someone sat with the specimen for a very long
time and got it right. The line quality is the argument. A wavering line says the draughtsman was
guessing; a line laid down once, at exactly the weight the form requires, says they knew. Nothing
here is sketched. Everything is *rendered* — meticulously, at the pace of someone who has done this
for thirty years and is in no hurry at all.

**Space is a vitrine.** The ground is not background; it is the inside of a case, and it is dark,
so that a pale specimen floats in it the way a thing floats in preserving fluid. The margin is wide
and it is inviolable — nothing crosses it, ever, and its width is the first thing the eye reads as
seriousness. Within that margin, objects sit on an invisible armature of thirds and halves, and
they sit *still*. There is no dynamism, no diagonal energy, no motion blur. A specimen at rest is
under control, and control is the point. Negative space is not emptiness but water: it has volume,
it presses in on the form, and it must be given at least as much of the plate as the subject is.
The one permitted violence is scale — a form allowed to run very large against a page of very small
marks, so that the eye falls a long way before it lands.

**Colour is withheld, and therefore lethal.** The palette is bone, ash, and a ground close enough to
black to swallow a careless mark. Onto that, exactly one chromatic accent, used at perhaps two
percent of the surface: an arterial note that appears only where the subject is alive, or wounded,
or watching. Because it appears so rarely, it becomes the plate's entire nervous system — the eye
hunts it, finds it at the eye of a thing, at a joint, at a temperature reading, and understands
without being told that this is where the specimen is dangerous. A second earth tone may stand in
for aged paper or specimen fluid, never for decoration. The material feel is engraved rather than
painted: hatching, stipple, the fine parallel rule of a burin. Fills are suspect. A form built from
five hundred deliberate strokes has a weight that no fill can counterfeit, and the difference is
visible at a glance even to somebody who cannot say why.

**Rhythm comes from repetition held just short of mechanism.** Rule ticks, specimen numbers,
hatching intervals, the beat of a grid of small figures — these accumulate into pattern, and pattern
is what rewards the second and third look. But every repeat is drawn, not stamped: the interval
breathes by a fraction, the hatch follows the form's curvature rather than the page's axis, the row
of small specimens varies in silhouette even where it repeats in placement. Perfect repetition
reads as printing. Repetition with a hand in it reads as devotion. The distinction is the whole
craft, and it must be honoured in every single element on the page — nothing may be duplicated
without being reconsidered.

**Hierarchy is by weight and isolation, never by size of type.** The principal specimen is the
largest, palest, most finely worked thing on the plate; everything else recedes into the ground by
losing contrast rather than by shrinking. Text is small, sparse, and set as though inked by the same
hand that drew the figure — a specimen name, a scale bar, a catalogue number, a phrase of six words
at most. It labels; it never explains. If a viewer needs a paragraph, the drawing has failed and the
paragraph will not save it. Type sits on the armature like everything else: aligned to the rules,
never floating, never overlapping a form, never crowding the margin. Letterforms are constructed —
built as strokes on a grid, of a piece with the rules and callouts — so that the page has one hand
throughout instead of a drawing and a font that met on it.

**The completed plate must survive being looked at closely for a long time.** That is the only test
that matters, and it is a hard one: it means no passage may be approximate, no corner may be filler,
no repeated element may be a copy. It means the tenth specimen in a grid takes as long as the first.
It means the marks that will be read as texture at arm's length must be correct as marks at reading
distance. Work at this standard is slow and it looks slow, and looking slow is precisely its value —
it announces, before a single subject is identified, that somebody at the very top of their craft
decided this was worth the hours. Anything less produces a diagram. The aim is an artefact.
